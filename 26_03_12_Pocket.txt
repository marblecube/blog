"Pocket"

Vidal Palacios | Claude > "Pocket"
Generative short fiction,
(2025)

## I

The planet Kythara turned its face to the strange accelerated heavens as it had for three hundred years, since the time of the Great Passage. To the Kytharans, it was simply the nature of things—the swift dance of the celestial sphere, where stars lived and died in the span of seasons, where the great wheel of the galaxy turned visibly overhead like some vast, luminous water-wheel.

Their capital, Vel'thyros, sprawled across seven hills, its white stone gleaming with an opalescent sheen that caught the racing starlight. In the Great Assembly, beneath columns that reflected the swift celestial dance, High Councilor Vex Nalthian addressed the gathered nobles about the new water-channels that would bring mountain streams to the eastern districts. The engineering was sound, he declared, built to last a thousand years—as all great works should be.

In the Observatory of Sacred Mysteries, the star-readers maintained their careful observations of the Swift Realm above. Chief Sky-Watcher Thylan Nexar had spent his lifetime cataloging the patterns of accelerated stellar evolution, developing elegant theories about how the gods lived in fast-time while mortals were blessed with contemplative time. His scrolls described the birth and death of star-clusters, the wheeling of galaxies, the gradual reddening of the cosmic background—all phenomena he attributed to divine will rather than physics.

The citizens of Kythara went about their lives with Roman confidence and pragmatism. They farmed their valleys, traded in their markets, built their roads and monuments. Children played in courtyards while overhead the universe aged in fast-forward, though to them it was as natural as sunrise. This was simply the way of things in the blessed realm of slow-time, where mortals could contemplate eternity while the gods raced through their celestial labors.

None suspected that their world was a temporal island, causally severed from the universe that spun past them in desperate acceleration, falling ever deeper into a gravitational well from which there could be no return.

## II

The changes began subtly, as such changes always do.

Kythul Theralus, master stonemason, first noticed it in the old monuments. The circular foundations of the Temple of Eternal Flame, laid by his grandfather's grandfather, were no longer quite round. The deviation was slight—perhaps a hand's width across the full diameter—but unmistakable to a craftsman's eye. He mentioned it to his apprentices, who shrugged and blamed the settling of ancient foundations.

The star-readers were the first to sense the deeper pattern. Young Zeth Stellan, grandson of the great Thylan Nexar, had inherited his grandfather's meticulous sky-charts spanning two centuries of observation. As he compared the recent celestial mappings with the ancient ones, he discovered a troubling inconsistency. The acceleration of the heavens appeared to be... accelerating. Where once a star's lifetime had passed in a full season, now the same cycle completed in mere months.

"The gods grow impatient," declared the High Diviner, but Zeth was not satisfied with theological explanations. There was something mechanical in the progression, something that suggested natural rather than divine causation.

In the countryside, farmers began reporting strange tidal effects in their irrigation channels. Water that had flowed predictably for generations now showed peculiar patterns—stronger flows toward the planetary south, weaker toward the north. The effect was minimal, barely perceptible, but consistent across the entire continent.

The great philosopher Mykandros, in his private gardens, contemplated these accumulated oddities while watching the racing cosmos overhead. In his journal, he wrote: "We are like passengers on a great ship, feeling the deck tilt beneath our feet, yet unable to see the vast ocean around us. The Swift Realm above grows ever swifter, while our own time seems to stretch like warm bronze. Are we falling toward something? Or is something falling toward us?"

Decades passed. The circular structures of the old city—the amphitheater, the great rotunda, the ancient walls—continued their slow distortion. Engineers attributed it to the natural settling of stone, though privately they wondered why the deformation always followed the same north-south pattern. The tidal effects in waterways became more pronounced. Children born in recent years seemed to grow differently—taller and thinner than their parents, as if stretched by some invisible hand.

The Swift Realm above raced ever faster. Entire galactic arms now wheeled visibly overhead in the span of a single night. The background glow of the heavens, once steady, now shifted through colors like a vast, slow aurora. Zeth Stellan, now old and bent, filled scroll after scroll with measurements that charted the quickening of the quickening, the deepening temporal gulf that surrounded their world.

Still, life continued. Lovers met beneath alien moons, poets composed verses about the beautiful racing heavens, children played games that mimicked the swift dance of stars. The ordinary business of civilization carried on, even as the very fabric of their world began its inexorable transformation.

## III

It was High Councilor Vex Nalthian the Younger—grandson of the water-channel builder—who first felt it in his bones.

He had risen early to address the Council about grain shortages in the southern provinces, but as he walked through the pre-dawn streets of Vel'thyros, something felt wrong with his very body. His legs seemed too long, his arms too thin. When he reached for the bronze door-handles of the Council chambers, his fingers appeared stretched, elongated, as if he were seeing himself in a curved mirror.

At first he thought it a trick of the racing starlight, but when he looked around the forum, he saw that all the vertical columns appeared slightly taller than they should be, while the horizontal steps seemed compressed. The great statue of the first Emperor, which had stood unchanged for four centuries, now towered impossibly tall and thin.

That same morning, across the city, others began to notice. Water no longer fell straight down from fountains but curved southward as it dropped. Birds struggled to maintain their flight patterns, as if fighting against an invisible current in the air itself. In the harbor, ships' masts swayed toward the south even in still air.

Chief Sky-Watcher Zeth Stellan, now ancient and nearly blind, was carried to his observatory tower for what would be his final observation. His assistants helped him focus the great bronze telescope on the Swift Realm above, where the universe now raced past in a blur of quickening time. Galaxies formed and dissolved in moments. The background glow of heaven shifted from red to deeper red to something beyond sight.

"We are falling," he whispered, his voice barely audible. "We have always been falling. The Swift Realm is not swift—we are slow. We are... outside of time, falling toward..."

He could not finish. The words for what lay ahead did not exist in any language.

In the countryside, farmers found their wells flowing strangely, the water pulled southward with increasing force. Ancient trees, their roots deep in Kytharan soil, began to lean and stretch. Children complained that their beds felt wrong, that sleeping had become uncomfortable, as if the very ground pulled at their bodies.

The Council convened in emergency session, but found themselves debating in a chamber where the ceiling seemed to press down while the walls stretched upward impossibly. High Councilor Nalthian, his body now visibly elongated, attempted to speak about the crisis, but his words seemed to stretch as well, deepening in pitch as he spoke.

In the Observatory of Sacred Mysteries, the star-readers offered prayers to gods who raced past in the quickening heavens above, but their voices too began to deepen and distort. Their ritual movements, practiced for centuries, now felt wrong, as if performed underwater.

The Swift Realm spun faster still. The last light of distant star-whirlpools faded into deep crimson and then to black. The background glow of heaven dimmed to darkness. Above the stretching world of Kythara, the universe aged toward its final night while below, the first citizens of Vel'thyros began to understand that their thousand-year-old civilization was approaching something that had been waiting for them all along—something patient and inexorable and vast beyond all reckoning.

The star-charts of Zeth Stellan scattered in the wind, his life's work of mapping the Swift Realm now meaningless as the boundary between slow-time and no-time began to dissolve. In the growing darkness above, the last light of creation wheeled toward its end, while below, the pocket universe of Kythara stretched toward its own impossible destiny.
